Los Angeles to San Francisco on I-5

The I-5 part of the drive runs about 311 miles, from downtown Los Angeles (exit 135) over the Grapevine and up the west side of the San Joaquin Valley to exit 446, where I-580 leaves for the Bay Area over Altamont Pass. Here is what you pass on the way north, in order, with the exit and which side to look.

Miles 135 to 140Boyle Heights to Elysian Park

The freeway threads the East Los Angeles Interchange in Boyle Heights, runs through Lincoln Heights, the city's first suburb, and crosses the Los Angeles River where the Arroyo Seco joins it, beside the wooded hills of Elysian Park.

  1. Exit 135 · on your left going north · History

    Yaanga and the Tongva

    Across the river, where downtown Los Angeles now stands, was Yaanga, recorded as the largest and most influential Tongva village in the region. At its center grew a great sycamore later called El Aliso, a landmark known to traders from as far away as present-day Yuma. The Portolá expedition reached Yaanga in 1769, and in 1781 Spanish settlers founded the pueblo of Los Angeles next to it.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 137 · History

    Arroyo Seco Parkway

    The freeway crosses the Los Angeles River here, where the Arroyo Seco flows in, and meets the Arroyo Seco Parkway, one of the oldest freeways in the United States. It was dedicated on December 30, 1940, and opened the next day, in time for crowds heading to the Rose Parade and Rose Bowl in Pasadena. It was the first road built under a 1939 California law that let the state limit access to a few set points.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 138 · on your left going north · History

    Elysian Park and Chavez Ravine

    The wooded hills beside the freeway are Elysian Park, created in 1886 and, at 600 acres, one of the largest parks in Los Angeles. Tucked into them is Chavez Ravine, home of Dodger Stadium. By the 1940s the ravine held three close-knit, mostly Mexican American neighborhoods, La Loma, Palo Verde and Bishop, where many families lived because of housing discrimination elsewhere in the city.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 140 to 145LA River and Griffith Park

The freeway follows the Los Angeles River through the Glendale Narrows, with the brushy hills of Griffith Park on the west side.

  1. Exit 141 · Land & water

    Los Angeles River

    The channel beside the freeway is the Los Angeles River. Most of it was lined with concrete after the deadly flood of 1938, but in this 11-mile stretch, the Glendale Narrows, the groundwater sits so high that the bottom was left unpaved. That is why willows, reeds and sandbars grow right in the river.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 141 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Griffith Park

    The brushy hills west of the freeway are Griffith Park, more than 4,000 acres of chaparral and parkland that the city calls the largest urban-wilderness municipal park in the United States. On December 16, 1896, Griffith J. Griffith gave the first 3,015 acres to the people of Los Angeles as a Christmas gift.

    Source: griffithobservatory.lacity.gov

  3. Exit 144 · on your left going north · History

    The 1933 Griffith Park fire

    On October 3, 1933, a brush fire in Griffith Park killed at least 29 men who volunteered or were ordered to fight it. Thousands of workers on a federally funded project were in the park that day, clearing brush and building trails for 40 cents an hour. The fire burned only about 47 acres, yet for 85 years it was the deadliest wildfire in California history.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 144 · Good stops

    Zoo, Autry and Travel Town

    Three family stops sit along the north side of Griffith Park: the Los Angeles Zoo, the Autry Museum of the American West, and Travel Town, a railroad museum with 43 full-size engines, cars and other rolling stock. Actor Gene Autry founded his museum of the West here in 1988.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 145 · on your right going north · History

    Grand Central Air Terminal

    Just east of the freeway stands the 1928 terminal building of Grand Central Airport, once a major gateway to Los Angeles by air. In July 1929 it became the western end of the Lindbergh Line, a 48-hour coast-to-coast trip with planes by day and trains by night. The old runway is now a city street, Grand Central Avenue.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 145 to 153Burbank and Sun Valley

Studios, the airport and the neighborhoods of Glendale, Burbank and Sun Valley spread across the flat east end of the San Fernando Valley, below the rocky Verdugo Mountains.

  1. Exit 146 · on your right going north · History

    Verdugo Mountains

    The rocky range rising east of the freeway is the Verdugo Mountains, topping out at 3,126 feet. They are named for José María Verdugo, a Spanish soldier who came north with the 1769 expedition and in 1784 received Rancho San Rafael, about 36,000 acres that now hold Glendale and Burbank.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 147 · Towns

    Burbank, the Media Capital

    Burbank, which the freeway passes through, is commonly known as the Media Capital of the World. Warner Bros. and the Walt Disney Company both have their headquarters here, in the Media District, a couple of miles west of the freeway.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 149 · on your left going north · History

    Lockheed's hidden factory

    During World War II, Lockheed's aircraft factory beside the Burbank airport was disguised from the air. It was covered with a huge painted tarp showing a quiet country neighborhood, with rubber cars and hundreds of fake trees made of chicken wire and feathers. Lockheed and its Vega company built 19,278 planes during the war.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 150 · on your left going north · Towns

    Sun Valley's thorny name

    Long before it was Sun Valley, this was Wixánga, a Fernandeño Tongva village whose name means something like place of the thorns, for the prickly pear cactus growing here. The Spanish name, Cañada de las Tunas, carried the same idea and survives in nearby La Tuna Canyon. Later the area was called Roscoe, and in 1950 residents chose the name Sun Valley.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 152 · Orchards & farms

    When the valley grew wheat

    In 1874, J. B. Lankershim and Isaac Van Nuys began dry-farming wheat across the southern half of the San Fernando Valley.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 153 to 162North San Fernando Valley

Pacoima, Mission Hills and Sylmar fill the north end of the valley, once a land of mission vineyards, wheat and olive groves, with the San Gabriel Mountains rising close by.

  1. Exit 155 · on your right going north · Towns

    Pacoima, Ritchie Valens' hometown

    Pacoima takes its name from Pakoinga, the name of a Native village here meaning entrance; Spanish ears heard its ng sound as an m. It is also the hometown of Ritchie Valens, born Richard Valenzuela here in 1941, who turned the Mexican folk song La Bamba into a rock and roll hit in 1958.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 158 · on your left going north · History

    The dam that almost failed

    The reservoirs just west of the freeway once supplied about 80 percent of Los Angeles' water. When the San Fernando earthquake struck on February 9, 1971, part of the Lower Van Norman Dam slid into its reservoir, leaving only about 5 feet of dam above the water. Eighty thousand people living below it were evacuated for days.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 159 · Orchards & farms

    Sylmar's sea of olives

    Sylmar's name, adopted in 1893, joins Latin words for forest and sea, for the sea of olive trees that covered these slopes. In 1894 up to 1,700 acres were planted, and by 1906 the groves were said to be the largest olive grove in the world. Today Sylmar is a suburb, the northernmost neighborhood in Los Angeles.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 159 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Power from the Columbia River

    Near the freeway sits the Sylmar Converter Station, the south end of the Pacific DC Intertie, a power line that runs 846 miles from the Columbia River near The Dalles, Oregon. The line carries direct current, and this station turns it into the alternating current Los Angeles uses, up to 3,100 megawatts.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 161 · on your right going north · History

    Los Angeles Aqueduct Cascades

    On the hillside beside the freeway, water from the eastern Sierra Nevada tumbles down the Cascades, the end of the Los Angeles Aqueduct. When the first water arrived on November 5, 1913, chief engineer William Mulholland said, "There it is. Take it."

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 162 · History

    Newhall Pass Interchange

    This tangle of ramps, where I-5 meets the Antelope Valley Freeway, is the Newhall Pass interchange, and parts of I-5 here are up to 21 lanes wide. Earthquakes knocked down ramps here twice, in 1971 and again in 1994. Officially it is the Clarence Wayne Dean Memorial Interchange, named for a police officer killed in the 1994 collapse.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 162 to 168Newhall Pass and Newhall

Newhall Pass, between the San Gabriel and Santa Susana Mountains, links the San Fernando Valley with the Santa Clarita Valley, where Newhall grew up as a railroad and oil town.

  1. Exit 162 · on your right going north · History

    Oak of the Golden Dream

    In Placerita Canyon, a few miles east, Francisco López made what the state's landmark plaque calls California's first authenticated gold discovery, on March 9, 1842. Gathering wild onions near an oak tree, he found gold flakes clinging to the onion roots. That was six years before the famous strike at Sutter's Mill, and the tree is now called the Oak of the Golden Dream.

    Source: ohp.parks.ca.gov

  2. Exit 163 · on your right going north · History

    Beale's Cut

    Before tunnels and freeways, stagecoaches crossed this pass through Beale's Cut, a slot dug through the mountain with picks and shovels and eventually deepened to 90 feet. Edward Beale, who won the right to collect tolls here in 1863, used Chinese immigrant workers to do most of the digging. Butterfield Overland Mail coaches running to San Francisco used the cut.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 167 · on your right going north · Good stops

    William S. Hart Park

    In Newhall, the ranch of silent film cowboy William S. Hart is now a 265-acre park with his Spanish-style mansion, trails and a barnyard of farm animals. Hart left it to Los Angeles County when he died in 1946.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 167 · on your left going north · History

    Pico Number 4 oil well

    Up Pico Canyon, west of the freeway, Well Number 4 struck oil on September 26, 1876, and became the first commercially successful oil well in the western United States. It kept producing for 114 years until it was capped in 1990, and it is often called the longest continuously operating oil well in the world.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 168 to 177Santa Clarita Valley

Planned neighborhoods of Valencia spread along the Santa Clara River, with Magic Mountain's roller coasters on the hills west of the freeway.

  1. Exit 169 · History

    Tataviam homeland

    The Santa Clarita Valley is considered the heart of the homeland of the Tataviam people, a name that means people on the south slope in the neighboring Kitanemuk language. They lived in about 20 villages in and around the valley, with summer and winter settlements, and harvested yucca and juniper berries. Their tribe today, the Fernandeño Tataviam Band of Mission Indians, is governed from San Fernando.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 170 · Land & water

    Santa Clara River

    The Santa Clara River, which passes under the freeway near Magic Mountain Parkway, runs 83 miles to the Pacific and is one of the least altered rivers in Southern California. Here its bed is dry most of the year. Spanish explorers named it on August 9, 1769, for Saint Clare of Assisi, and the valley later became known as little Santa Clara, Santa Clarita, to tell it apart from Santa Clara in Northern California.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 170 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Six Flags Magic Mountain

    The roller coaster tracks twisting over the hills west of the freeway belong to Six Flags Magic Mountain, which opened on May 29, 1971. In 2022 it became the first amusement park to offer 20 roller coasters. Several have closed since, but it still has one of the biggest collections of coasters anywhere.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 172 · on your left going north · History

    The Death Valley rescue

    Near where Highway 126 meets the freeway stood the adobe headquarters of Rancho San Francisco, first built about 1804 as a granary for Mission San Fernando. In January 1850, William Lewis Manly and John Rogers reached it and got supplies and animals to rescue their gold-seeking emigrant party, stranded and starving in Death Valley about 250 miles northeast.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 172 · on your right going north · History

    St. Francis Dam disaster

    Just before midnight on March 12, 1928, the St. Francis Dam failed in San Francisquito Canyon, about 10 miles north of Santa Clarita. The flood tore down the canyon and along the Santa Clara River to the sea, killing at least 431 people.

    Source: damfailures.org

  6. Exit 173 · on your left going north · History

    Val Verde, the Black Palm Springs

    In the hills west of the freeway, Black investors led by Sidney Dones founded a resort community in 1924, when African Americans were often barred from public beaches and pools. It became known as the Black Palm Springs, and Ella Fitzgerald and Lena Horne appeared there. Today it is the community of Val Verde, Spanish for green valley.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 177 to 191Castaic and the Five Mile Grade

North of Castaic, the long Five Mile Grade leads into the Angeles National Forest, where the freeway crosses the Sierra Pelona Mountains between Castaic Lake and Pyramid Lake.

  1. Exit 176 · Oddities

    Five Mile Grade crossover

    On the Five Mile Grade north of Castaic, the two sides of the freeway trade places, so for about 5 miles traffic keeps to the left. When I-5 was rebuilt in the 1960s, engineers put the uphill and downhill lanes on separate hillsides, and the downhill lanes got a gentler grade to cut down on runaway trucks.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 176 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Castaic Lake and its name

    Castaic takes its name from the Chumash word Kaštiq, meaning the eye. Above the town, Castaic Dam, finished in 1973, rises 340 feet and stretches nearly a mile, holding Castaic Lake at the end of the West Branch of the California Aqueduct. Water from Northern California rests here before flowing on to millions of people around Los Angeles.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 183 · History

    The old Ridge Route

    East of the freeway, the original Ridge Route of 1915 winds along the ridgetops from Castaic toward Gorman. It was the first paved highway linking Los Angeles directly with the San Joaquin Valley, and between Castaic and Gorman its 697 turns added up to about 97 complete circles in 36 miles. Between Castaic and Quail Lake the speed limit was 15 miles an hour.

    Source: ridgeroute.org

  4. Exit 191 · on your left going north · History

    Highway 99 under the lake

    Beneath Pyramid Lake lies a drowned highway. In 1933 the three-lane Ridge Route Alternate, later US 99, opened through the canyon of Piru Creek, with a middle lane that drivers in both directions used for passing, nicknamed the suicide lane. After Pyramid Dam was finished, rising water covered the old road, and by 1972 it was fully under the lake.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 191 · on your left going north · Good stops

    Vista del Lago

    The Vista del Lago Visitors Center, off its own exit, sits above Pyramid Lake with an outdoor overlook and exhibits from the California Department of Water Resources. The lake holds about 180,000 acre-feet of State Water Project water, pumped up from the San Joaquin Valley and over the Tehachapi Mountains.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 191 to 203Pyramid Lake to Gorman

Pyramid Lake fills the canyon of Piru Creek west of the freeway in the Sierra Pelona Mountains, and farther north the land opens into Peace Valley around Gorman.

  1. Exit 198 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Quail Lake on the fault

    Quail Lake, along Highway 138 east of the freeway, lies in the rift zone of the San Andreas Fault and stores water for the West Branch of the California Aqueduct. Water pumped up to the lake flows by gravity through a pipeline to a power plant at Pyramid Lake. The fault itself slices right through Gorman.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 202 · Towns

    Gorman and its wildflowers

    In spring after a wet winter, California poppies and lupine can blanket the hills around Gorman. People have stopped here for centuries: it was the Tataviam village of Kulshra'jek, a trading crossroads, and a local historian calls it one of the oldest continuously used roadside rest stops in California. In 2005 Gorman had only 15 homes.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 203 to 210Tejon Pass and Lebec

Tejon Pass sits among the open hills of Tejon Ranch around Lebec.

  1. Exit 204 · Land & water

    Tejon Pass summit

    Near the top of Tejon Pass, I-5 reaches 4,144 feet, its highest point in California, close to the line between Los Angeles and Kern counties. The pass sits where California's two largest faults, the San Andreas and the Garlock, meet. Tejón is Spanish for badger, though the dead badger behind the name was found in 1806 at a different canyon, about 15 miles northeast.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 205 · on your left going north · History

    The Brink's jewelry heist

    At 2 in the morning on July 11, 2022, a Brink's tractor-trailer carrying jewelry from a show in San Mateo to one in Pasadena pulled into a truck stop at the Frazier Mountain Park Road exit. One guard went inside to eat while the other slept in the cab. Within 27 minutes, thieves cut the trailer's padlock and made off with about two dozen of its 73 bags of jewelry.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 205 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Frazier Mountain

    The broad, pine-covered peak west of the pass is Frazier Mountain, 8,017 feet high. To the Chumash people it is Toshololo, the mountain of the east, named for its place east of Mount Pinos and tied to the morning star and the spring equinox. Its English name honors William Frazer, a miner who worked here in the 1850s.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 207 · Orchards & farms

    Tejon Ranch cattle

    The open hills around Lebec are part of Tejon Ranch, more than 270,000 acres and the largest single piece of private land in California. Cattle graze about 250,000 acres of it, up to 12,000 head depending on the season. The ranch says you know winter is over when orange poppies spill across the hills and young calves trail their mothers.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 207 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Castac Lake, the eye

    East of Lebec lies Castac Lake, a natural salty lake that formed about 10,000 years ago when the fan of sediment washed down by Cuddy Creek dammed a valley along the Garlock Fault. The Chumash, Kitanemuk and Yokuts peoples all named it for an eye.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 210 to 215Grapevine Canyon

Grapevine Canyon carries the freeway's long, steep grade past Fort Tejon's centuries-old valley oaks to the southern edge of the San Joaquin Valley.

  1. Exit 210 · on your left going north · History

    Fort Tejon

    The adobe buildings among the oaks beside the freeway are Fort Tejon, an Army post from 1854 to 1864 on the main route between the Central Valley and Southern California. From 1858 it was the western end of the Army's experiment with camels as pack animals. The soldiers found the camels hardy but temperamental, and they spooked the cavalry's horses.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 210 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Peter Lebeck's oak

    On the fort grounds is the grave of Peter Lebeck, buried under a valley oak in 1837. Someone carved his epitaph into the tree: Peter Lebeck, killed by a X bear, October 17, 1837. Early settlers called grizzlies X bears, probably for a dark pattern on their backs, and he is the earliest known victim of a bear attack in California.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 210 · Land & water

    The Grapevine grade

    This steep stretch through Grapevine Canyon is the Grapevine itself, a 6 percent grade that runs 5 miles and drops about 1,600 feet. Escape ramps branch off the downhill lanes for big rigs whose brakes fail, and in hot weather car engines often overheat on the long uphill lanes.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 215 · History

    California's first reservation

    These mountains are ancestral homeland of the Tejon Indian Tribe, a federally recognized tribe of Kitanemuk, Yokuts, Paiute and Chumash people based in Bakersfield. In 1853 they became the site of California's first Indian reservation, the Sebastian, or Tejon, Reservation, which reached from Tejon Canyon west to Grapevine Canyon. The state's historical marker says the number of people living there varied from 500 to 2,000.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 215 to 221Wheeler Ridge and the valley's edge

Below the Grapevine the land flattens at Wheeler Ridge, where the Tehachapi and San Emigdio mountains meet the floor of the San Joaquin Valley and Highway 99 meets I-5.

  1. Exit 215 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Water lifted over the mountains

    About 6 miles east of here, the Edmonston Pumping Plant lifts California Aqueduct water 1,926 feet up and over the Tehachapi Mountains toward Southern California. Apart from pumped-storage power plants, it is the most powerful water-lifting system in the world. At full capacity its pumps can send nearly 2 million gallons a minute uphill.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 215 · Land & water

    Where the valley begins

    At the foot of the Grapevine, the freeway reaches the southern tip of the San Joaquin Valley, about 1,500 feet above sea level. Between Tejon Pass and here, the road changes elevation by more than 2,600 feet in about 12 miles. From here the Central Valley, broad and flat, runs about 450 miles between the Coast Ranges and the Sierra Nevada.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 219 · Land & water

    Wheeler Ridge, a growing fold

    The long, low ridge where the mountains meet the valley floor here is Wheeler Ridge, a fold in the earth's crust that is still growing. It is being pushed up over a thrust fault, and geologists have found that the fold is getting longer at its eastern end, toward the east-southeast.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 221 · Land & water

    The 1952 earthquake

    At 4:52 in the morning on July 21, 1952, the White Wolf Fault broke near Wheeler Ridge in a magnitude 7.3 earthquake, the strongest in California since 1906. It killed 12 people, and the mountain town of Tehachapi was hit hardest.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 221 to 234The vanished southern lakes

Flat farmland covers the old beds of Kern Lake and Buena Vista Lake, with the San Emigdio Mountains forming the valley's southern wall and the oil country of Maricopa and Taft off to the west.

  1. Exit 221 · History

    Start of the West Side Freeway

    Highway 99 and I-5 meet at the Wheeler Ridge interchange. From here to Woodland, I-5 is the West Side Freeway. It was chosen as the Interstate route because it skips the valley's big cities, such as Bakersfield and Fresno, for a faster, more direct trip.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 225 · on your right going north · History

    James Dean's speeding ticket

    On September 30, 1955, the actor James Dean was driving north on Highway 99, on his way to a sports car race in Salinas, when a Highway Patrol officer pulled him over near here and wrote him a speeding ticket. The officer listed the place as Mettler Station. Later that same day, Dean was killed in a crash farther along his trip.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 225 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Wind Wolves Preserve

    In the hills southwest of here lies Wind Wolves Preserve, 93,000 acres of grassland, oak and canyon that its owner, The Wildlands Conservancy, calls the largest nonprofit nature preserve on the West Coast. The name describes tall grass bending and parting in gusts of wind, like unseen animals running across the slopes. In 1998, 19 tule elk were released here, and the herd has grown to about 500.

    Source: wildlandsconservancy.org

  4. Exit 225 · on your left going north · History

    The Lakeview Gusher

    Near Maricopa, in March 1910, an oil well called Lakeview Number One blew out and would not stop. For 18 months it gushed, about 9 million barrels in all, and it is often called the largest accidental oil spill in history. The force of the flow destroyed the derrick.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 228 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Mountains named for a saint

    The mountains forming the valley's southern wall are the San Emigdio Mountains, which top out at 7,492 feet. They are named for Saint Emygdius, an early Christian martyr whom Catholics call on for protection from earthquakes.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 230 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Kern Lake's empty bed

    The flat fields near the freeway here were once Kern Lake, the smallest of three big lakes at the south end of the valley. It was the first lake the Kern River fed. From Kern Lake, water ran west through a channel called Connecting Slough into Buena Vista Lake, and in wet years the two joined into one large lake.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 234 to 246Kern County orchards

Kern County's valley floor here is farm country, where almonds and pistachios are among the most valuable crops, and Highways 119 and 43 lead west to the oil towns of Taft and Maricopa.

  1. Exit 239 · Orchards & farms

    Kern County nut country

    Many of the neat rows of trees in Kern County are almonds and pistachios. In 2022 the county harvested about 223,000 acres of almonds and 158,000 acres of pistachios, more pistachio acres than any other county in California. Together those two nuts were worth about 1.

    Source: nass.usda.gov

  2. Exit 244 · on your left going north · Towns

    Taft, once called Moron

    Highway 119 leads to Taft, an oil town at the valley's southwest edge. The town's oil museum tells how locals asked the railroad to name its station Moro, but the railroad worried it would be confused with Morro Bay and named it Moron instead, back when the word did not yet mean anything unkind. In 1909 the town took a new name, for President William Howard Taft.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 244 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Buena Vista Lake

    Southwest of here, much of the farmland was once Buena Vista Lake, fed by the Kern River and the second largest of the lakes in the Tulare Basin. On its south shore stood Tulamniu, a Yokuts village lived in for several centuries, which the Spanish commander Pedro Fages named Buena Vista in 1772. Two small lakes, Lake Webb and Lake Evans, now sit on part of the old bed.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 246 to 262Kern River and the Elk Hills

The freeway crosses the Kern River near the Kern Water Bank's recharge basins and runs past the low, bare Elk Hills.

  1. Exit 246 · Land & water

    Crossing the Kern River

    The freeway crosses the Kern River here, though this far downstream its channel is often dry. John C. Frémont named the river in 1845 for Edward Kern, the mapmaker of his expedition, who, as the story goes, nearly drowned in its rushing water.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 246 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Kern Water Bank

    Near here, the Kern Water Bank covers about 32 square miles of the southern San Joaquin Valley. In wet years, water is spread into more than 80 basins and slowly soaks into the ground, where it is saved for dry years. It can store 1.

    Source: kwb.org

  3. Exit 253 · on your left going north · Good stops

    Tule Elk State Natural Reserve

    A few miles southwest, Tule Elk State Natural Reserve keeps a herd of about 30 to 35 tule elk on nearly 1,000 acres, watched from a viewing platform. These elk are found only in California. About half a million once roamed the state, but by the 1870s they were thought to be extinct, until a few were found hiding in the tules near Buena Vista Lake.

    Source: parks.ca.gov

  4. Exit 253 · on your left going north · History

    Elk Hills and Teapot Dome

    The low hills to the west are the Elk Hills, over one of the most productive oil fields in the country. In 1912 President Taft made them the nation's first Naval Petroleum Reserve, oil saved for the Navy. In 1922 Interior Secretary Albert Fall leased them, without competitive bidding, to oilman Edward Doheny, who had quietly lent Fall 100,000 dollars with no interest.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 257 · History

    Henry Miller, the Cattle King

    Buttonwillow was a headquarters of Miller and Lux, the company of Henry Miller, known as the Cattle King of California. Miller came west in 1850 as a butcher and teamed up with another German immigrant, Charles Lux, in 1858. At its height the company owned about 1.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 262 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Midway Substation

    The big electrical yard about a mile east of Buttonwillow is Midway Substation. Here Southern California Edison's power lines, which cross the mountains to the south, meet PG&E's grid. In winter, power often flows north toward the Pacific Northwest, where people run heaters; in summer it flows south for air conditioners.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 262 · on your left going north · Towns

    The Buttonwillow tree

    The town of Buttonwillow is named for a single tree, and it is not a willow at all. It is a buttonbush. A lone one here stood out as a landmark on an old trail across the valley, and it was a Yokuts meeting place long before settlers held stock rodeos beside it.

    Source: ohp.parks.ca.gov

Miles 262 to 278Buttonwillow cotton country

Around Buttonwillow, known locally as cotton country, farm fields and big power lines spread across the valley floor, with the Temblor Range on the far western skyline.

  1. Exit 262 · on your right going north · Oddities

    California's balance point

    About 7 miles northeast of here lies the center of population of California. The Census Bureau finds it by imagining a flat, weightless map of the state with every resident given the same weight, then finding the point where the map would balance. Since 1970, that point has stayed within about 10 miles of Buttonwillow.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 263 · on your left going north · Land & water

    McKittrick Tar Pits

    West of Buttonwillow, near the tiny town of McKittrick, natural asphalt oozes out of the ground from the oil field below. Like the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles, these pits trapped animals during the ice ages, from about 15,000 to 50,000 years ago. Fossils found here include saber-toothed cats, short-faced bears, American lions, ground sloths, bison and mammoths.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 268 · Orchards & farms

    Cotton country

    Cotton has deep roots on this side of the valley. Sandy loam soils, a long growing season without rain, big irrigation systems and relative freedom from pests like the boll weevil made the San Joaquin Valley ideal cotton land. A strain called Acala, developed at the cotton research station in nearby Shafter, became so important that state laws passed in 1925 made it the only cotton allowed in key areas.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 268 · on your left going north · Land & water

    The Temblor Range

    On clear days, the mountains along the far western skyline are the Temblor Range, named from the Spanish word for tremor. The San Andreas Fault runs along the foot of their far side, at the edge of the Carrizo Plain. The peaks average about 3,500 feet, and the highest, McKittrick Summit, reaches 4,331 feet.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 273 · Land & water

    San Joaquin kit fox

    This dry west side of the valley is home to the San Joaquin kit fox, an endangered fox with huge ears. Kit foxes are the smallest wild members of the dog family in North America, and an adult weighs only about 5 pounds. It hunts mostly at night, mainly for kangaroo rats.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 275 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Downhill all the way

    A few miles west, the California Aqueduct flows downhill by gravity, with no pumps. From the Coastal Branch split south of Kettleman City, its water runs about 66 miles to the Buena Vista Pumping Plant, the first of four plants that lift it toward and over the Tehachapi Mountains. The canal is built on a slight grade so gravity does the work.

    Source: Wikipedia

Oil pumps dot the low Lost Hills west of the freeway, the aqueduct runs past the town of Lost Hills, and Kern National Wildlife Refuge lies out on the flats to the east.

  1. Exit 278 · on your left going north · History

    Blackwells Corner

    West on Highway 46, where it meets Highway 33, Blackwells Corner is named for George Blackwell, who started a rest stop there in 1921. James Dean stopped there in 1955, and it is often described as the last place he was seen alive before his fatal crash near Cholame.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 278 · on your left going north · Towns

    Lost Hills

    Lost Hills, a town of about 2,400 people a couple of miles west of the freeway, takes its name from the low Lost Hills range beside it. Many residents work for the Wonderful Company, whose pistachio and almond processing plant is here. Each fall, pistachio trees are shaken and the nuts hauled to processing plants; at the one in Lost Hills, workers sort them by hand.

  3. Exit 280 · on your left going north · History

    Lost Hills oil field

    In July 1910, two drillers named Martin and Dudley were drilling a water well for livestock in the low hills west of here. At 530 feet they struck oil instead, and the Lost Hills oil field was born. The hills rise barely 200 feet above the valley, and so much oil has been pumped out that parts of them have sunk as much as 8 feet.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 284 · Land & water

    Tule fog season

    From November through March, tule fog can fill this southern end of the valley. The thick ground fog, named for the tule marshes of the Central Valley, tends to last longer in the south and east of the valley than farther north. Inside it, visibility is usually less than 600 feet, and it can drop to almost nothing within a few feet.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 288 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Kern National Wildlife Refuge

    East of here, Kern National Wildlife Refuge protects 11,249 acres of desert uplands, an old river corridor and marsh on the southern edge of what was once Tulare Lake. In winter its wetlands shelter migrating ducks, geese and other water birds that travel the Pacific Flyway.

    Source: fws.gov

Miles 292 to 309Into Kings County

North of the Kern County line, the old bed of Tulare Lake, now farmland, spreads east of the freeway.

  1. Exit 293 · History

    Kern and Kings county line

    The line between Kern and Kings counties crosses the freeway here. Kern County, formed in 1866, takes its name from the Kern River. Kings County, carved out of Tulare County in 1893, takes its name from the Kings River, which Spanish soldiers called the River of the Holy Kings.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 296 · Land & water

    Valley fever in the dust

    The soils of the San Joaquin Valley, especially its southern end, can hold Coccidioides, a fungus that causes valley fever, also called San Joaquin Valley fever. Its spores rise into the air when dusty ground is stirred up by wind, farming or construction, and people catch it by breathing them in. It is not contagious, and about 60 percent of people who are infected have few or no symptoms.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 300 · on your left going north · Oddities

    The Coastal Branch

    A few miles west of here, a branch of the California Aqueduct splits off and heads for the coast. The Coastal Branch runs 116 miles, climbing through five pumping plants to deliver water to San Luis Obispo, Santa Maria and Santa Barbara.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 305 · on your right going north · Towns

    Alpaugh and Atwell's Island

    Utica Avenue leads east to Alpaugh, a town on what was once Atwell's Island, the largest island in Tulare Lake. It was first known as Hog-Root Island, and a Visalia lawyer named Allen Atwell raised hogs on it. The island was a regular stop for the lake's commercial ferry.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 305 · on your right going north · History

    Colonel Allensworth's town

    About 30 miles east, past Alpaugh, Colonel Allensworth State Historic Park preserves the first California town founded, financed and governed by African Americans. Army veteran Allen Allensworth and four partners started it in 1908, hoping to build a Tuskegee of the West, a place where Black families could own land, farm and run their own affairs.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 309 · Oddities

    Crossing the California Aqueduct

    The freeway crosses the California Aqueduct here, the concrete river that carries water from the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta toward Southern California. Counting from the south, this is the second of I-5's five crossings of the aqueduct. A typical stretch is 40 feet wide at the bottom and holds water about 30 feet deep, and the whole aqueduct runs more than 400 miles.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 309 to 319Kettleman Hills

The Kettleman Hills, with their oil field, rise west of the freeway.

  1. Exit 309 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Ghost of Tulare Lake

    East of here lies the bed of Tulare Lake, once the largest freshwater lake west of the Mississippi River. The Tachi Yokut Tribe calls it Pa'ashi, big water. The rivers that fed it were diverted for farming, and by 1899 it was dry except for marshes.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 309 · on your left going north · History

    A landfill and a town's fight

    Off Highway 41, about 3.5 miles southwest of Kettleman City, the Kettleman Hills facility is a landfill of about 1,500 acres for hazardous and household waste, run by a subsidiary of Waste Management. It opened in the late 1970s.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 309 · on your right going north · Good stops

    Kettleman City, halfway

    Kettleman City is near the halfway point between Los Angeles and San Francisco or Sacramento, which makes this exit a major stop for food and lodging. The travel stops cluster at the Highway 41 junction, while the town itself, about 1,200 people, sits about a mile north, past the aqueduct. It lies only about half a mile north of the 36th parallel.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 314 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Kettleman Hills

    The long, low hills along the west side are the Kettleman Hills, about 30 miles long and up to about 1,200 feet high. They are named, with a spelling change, for Dave Kettelman, who grazed sheep and cattle here in the 1860s. They are a long fold in the rock, squeezed up by tectonic forces, and they run parallel to the San Andreas Fault, farther west.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 319 · on your left going north · History

    The 1928 Kettleman gusher

    In October 1928, after 19 months of drilling, the Milham Exploration Company's Elliott Number 1 well struck oil more than 7,000 feet under the Kettleman Hills. It blew out with a roar heard 20 miles away, spraying oil so light that it was white, and it reportedly could run a car without refining. The Kettleman North Dome became one of the most productive oil fields in the country.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 319 to 330Fresno County's West Side

The freeway runs through the West Side farmland of Fresno County, which in 2022 led California counties in the value of its almonds, pistachios, garlic and processing tomatoes.

  1. Exit 319 · on your left going north · Towns

    Avenal, the oat field

    Highway 269 crosses the Kettleman Hills to Avenal, whose name is Spanish for oat field. Spanish soldiers and explorers named it for the wild oats, waist high, that covered the plain like golden silk. Standard Oil laid out the town in 1929, after oil was found in the hills, and its first post office was a cigar box on the counter of the general store.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 322 · Orchards & farms

    Garlic country

    Fresno County grows most of the garlic in the United States, and west side towns such as Huron picked up the slack as Gilroy, once the country's biggest garlic producer, ran out of room. In 2022 the county harvested about 23,000 acres and 185,000 tons of garlic, nearly two-thirds of California's crop, worth about 350 million dollars.

    Source: abc30.com

  3. Exit 325 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Gates Substation bottleneck

    The sprawling electrical yard east of the freeway is Gates Substation, a key junction on the power lines linking Northern and Southern California. Between here and Los Banos, the corridor once had only two of its biggest lines. That bottleneck limited how much power could flow, and it was one of the leading causes of the California electricity crisis of 2000 and 2001.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 325 · on your right going north · Towns

    Huron, harvest town

    East of the freeway is Huron, a farm town of about 6,200 people that swells to more than 15,000 at harvest time, when migrant farmworkers arrive. It began in 1888 as a water stop on the Southern Pacific Railroad. In the 2000 census, Huron had the highest share of Hispanic or Latino residents of any city in the United States.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 327 · History

    Arroyo Pasajero bridges

    The freeway bridges here cross Los Gatos Creek, once called Arroyo Pasajero, the travelers' creek. On March 10, 1995, extreme rain sent fast floodwater down the creek. The flood scoured the creek bed and undermined the bridge piers, both I-5 bridges failed, and four cars and a truck went into the creek.

    The Harris Ranch restaurant opened beside the freeway in 1977, aimed at local farmers, and soon became a popular stop for travelers on the busy freeway between Los Angeles and San Francisco.   2. Exit 334 · on your left going north · History

    Coalinga Oil Field

    The 1983 Coalinga earthquake

    Harris Ranch feedlot

    Harris Ranch is California's largest beef producer, and its feedlot sits right beside the freeway.     On January 28, 1948, a chartered DC-3 crashed in the Diablo Range about 20 miles west of Coalinga, near Los Gatos Creek. All 32 people aboard died. Most were Mexican farmworkers being sent back to Mexico, some at the end of their bracero contracts.

    Mendota, cantaloupe town

    Highway 33 leads from here to Mendota, known as the Cantaloupe Center of the World.   6. Exit 353 · Orchards & farms

    The nation's top farm county

    Fresno County, which the freeway crosses here, was the number one farm county in the nation in 2017, when its crops and livestock were worth almost 8 billion dollars. About 1.9 million acres, nearly half the county, are farmed.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 355 · Orchards & farms

    The farm water signs

    Starting around 2008, big signs went up in fields along I-5, with slogans like Congress Created Dust Bowl and No Water Equals No Jobs. The aqueduct runs through flat farmland, with the San Joaquin River and the town of Firebaugh well off to the east and the Panoche Hills to the west.

  1. Exit 372 · Land & water

    Panoche Creek and the mercury mines

    Panoche Creek, which comes out of the hills near here, drains a larger area than any other stream on the Diablo Range's eastern slope. Its old Spanish name was Arroyo de Panoche Grande, Big Sugarloaf Creek. It was part of a route between Native settlements on the central coast and the valley, and a watering place on El Camino Viejo.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 379 · on your left going north · Good stops

    Mercey Hot Springs

    On Little Panoche Road, in the hills west of the freeway, Mercey Hot Springs has been a resort since the early 1900s. Water flows from one spring at 119 degrees Fahrenheit, and the resort has cabins, campsites, bathtub soaking pools and a swimming pool fed by a hot artesian well.

  3. Exit 382 · History

    The San Luis Canal

    The big canal on this part of the drive is officially the San Luis Canal, the federally built section of the California Aqueduct.     Firebaugh's Ferry

    East of the freeway, on the San Joaquin River, is Firebaugh, named for Andrew Firebaugh, a Virginian who ran a ferry across the river during the Gold Rush.     Caltrans rebuilt both sides of the John Chuck Erreca Rest Area in a 30 million dollar project, reopening one side in August 2025 and the other in May 2026. Caltrans calls it one of the most sustainable rest areas it has ever built, with utilities designed to use little water and power.

    East of the freeway, the San Joaquin River winds past Firebaugh. The southern Yokuts name for it, Tihshachu, means salmon-spearing place.     Beside the freeway, the Dos Amigos Pumping Plant lifts California Aqueduct water 118 feet.     East of the freeway lie the Grasslands, about 250 square miles of wetlands and grasslands along the San Joaquin River, named a wetland of international importance under the Ramsar treaty.     Two canals side by side

    Two big canals run side by side along the western edge of the valley between here and Tracy: the state's California Aqueduct and the federal Delta-Mendota Canal.     Kesterson and the selenium birds

    About 11 miles east is Kesterson Reservoir, now part of San Luis National Wildlife Refuge.     The town of Gustine, east of the freeway, is named for Sara Miller, the daughter of cattle king Henry Miller.     About 7 miles east, where the Merced River meets the San Joaquin, stood Hills Ferry, a river crossing started in 1850 and taken over by Jesse Hill in 1854.   5. Exit 423 · on your right going north · Towns

    Newman and Orestimba Creek

    The town of Newman, east of the freeway, was founded in 1888 by Simon Newman, a businessman who came to California from Bavaria as a teenager in 1862. Born Simon Neumann, he arrived speaking only German and Hebrew, learned English, and translated his family name.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 426 · Orchards & farms

    Almond bloom

    Almonds are the top farm product of Stanislaus County, which the freeway crosses here. Each February, the pollination of California's almonds is the largest managed pollination event in the world: more than a million beehives, nearly half of all the hives in the country, are trucked in to the orchards.

    Crows Landing airfield

    East of the freeway is the old Crows Landing airfield. The Navy broke ground on December 1, 1942, and trained pilots here in Wildcat and Avenger warplanes during World War Two, later adding big patrol bombers.     On clear days a lone, double-peaked mountain stands on the northwest skyline: Mount Diablo, 3,849 feet tall and about 40 miles away.     The I-580 junction

    At this junction, I-580 heads for the Bay Area over Altamont Pass, a major crossing of the Diablo Range. In all, I-580 runs about 76 miles, from San Rafael through Oakland to this meeting with I-5 south of Tracy. Together with I-5, it is a main route between the Bay Area and Southern California.
