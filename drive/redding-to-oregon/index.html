Redding to Oregon on I-5: Mount Shasta and the Siskiyous

From Redding (exit 677) to the Oregon line is about 120 miles of I-5 and the steepest, most scenic part of the road in California: over Shasta Lake, up the Sacramento River canyon, past Mount Shasta and into the Siskiyous. Here is what you pass on the way north, in order, with the exit and which side to look.

Miles 677 to 680Redding

Redding sits at the northwestern end of the Central Valley, with mountains on the north, east and west and farmland to the south.

  1. Exit 677 · History

    The Carr Fire

    On July 26, 2018, the Carr Fire jumped the Sacramento River into the west side of Redding, and 38,000 people had to evacuate. That evening a fire whirl formed, with winds estimated above 143 miles an hour, as strong as an EF3 tornado, and it rose an estimated 18,000 feet into the sky.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 678 · on your left going north · Towns

    Redding, once Poverty Flats

    During the Gold Rush, miners found little gold in this ground, and the spot was called Poverty Flats. In 1868 the Central Pacific Railroad's land agent, Benjamin Redding, bought land here for a northern terminus, and the railroad built a town and named it for him. The tracks arrived in 1872.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 678 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Sundial Bridge

    West of the freeway, the Sundial Bridge, a 700-foot footbridge designed by Santiago Calatrava, crosses the Sacramento River. Its 217-foot mast points due north at a 42-degree angle and casts its shadow on a giant dial. The tip of that shadow moves about a foot a minute, so you can watch the Earth turn.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 680 to 688North Redding and the dam towns

Here Redding's northern edge meets the city of Shasta Lake, with Shasta Dam a few miles west.

  1. Exit 680 · on your left going north · History

    Old Shasta

    Highway 299 leads to Shasta, about six miles west of Redding. In the 1850s it was the largest town in the area and the county seat, home to some 3,500 people. Then in 1873 the railroad bypassed it for Redding, and Shasta faded into a near ghost town.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 682 · Oddities

    The 9.5-mile conveyor belt

    To make concrete for Shasta Dam, builders needed mountains of sand and gravel. So they built what was then the largest conveyor belt system in the world, 9.5 miles long, running from Redding to the dam site. It could carry 1,100 tons of material an hour.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 684 · on your left going north · Towns

    Shasta Lake, the dam towns

    The city of Shasta Lake grew out of five small communities that sprang up in 1938, when work began on Shasta Dam: Central Valley, Project City, Summit City, Pine Grove and Toyon. Pine Grove stood where Pine Grove Avenue now meets the freeway. The communities did not join into one city until 1993.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 685 · on your left going north · History

    Shasta Dam

    Shasta Dam stands about four miles west, 602 feet tall. When it was finished in 1945, 26 months earlier than planned, only Hoover Dam was taller. During World War II, with so many men gone to fight, many of the workers were women and high school students.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 687 · on your left going north · History

    Kennett, the drowned town

    Under Shasta Lake lies Kennett, once the largest mining town in the area outside Redding and Shasta. Its two biggest mines held unusually pure copper ore. There's no record of any public hearing to ask residents before it was flooded.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 688 to 704Shasta Lake

The freeway crosses Shasta Lake, whose steep shores are covered with tall evergreens and manzanita.

  1. Exit 688 · Oddities

    The drowned highway

    Before the lake, old Highway 99 ran through Mountain Gate and then plunged into the valley that Shasta Lake now fills. In drought years, when the water falls, old bridges, road cuts and guardrails come back into view.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 690 · History

    Bass Hill stage road

    Near the Bridge Bay exit stands a marker to the stagecoach days. The California and Oregon stage road crossed the top of Bass Hill here and dropped down to the Pit River, and the hill was a favorite spot for bandit holdups. The marker honors the stage drivers under the title Pioneers Who Held the Ribbons.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 689 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Shasta Lake

    Shasta Lake is California's largest reservoir, with 365 miles of steep, wooded shoreline. It has four big arms, made by the Sacramento River, the McCloud River, the Pit River and Sulanharas Creek. At its deepest, it's 517 feet.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 691 · History

    Pit River Bridge

    The Pit River Bridge is a double-decker. I-5 rides the top deck and the railroad runs underneath. Built in 1942, it stands about 500 feet above the old bed of the Pit River, making it the highest double-deck bridge in the United States.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 693 · Oddities

    The giant median

    Just north of the Pit River Bridge, the two directions of I-5 split apart, leaving what is said to be the second-largest median in California. Only Interstate 8's In-Ko-Pah grade has a bigger one.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 694 · Land & water

    Shasta salamanders

    The limestone hills around this lake hide salamanders found nowhere else on Earth. Scientists once thought there was just one kind. In 2018 they found three, including the Wintu Shasta salamander, which lives on the lake's north shore near the McCloud River.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 695 · on your right going north · Good stops

    Lake Shasta Caverns

    Getting to these limestone caves takes a catamaran ride across the lake's McCloud arm and then a bus ride up a steep mountain road. The caves sit about 900 feet above the lake. Inside, the words James Richardson wrote on the wall on November 11, 1878, claiming the discovery, are still clearly legible.

    Source: lakeshastacaverns.com

  8. Exit 696 · History

    Winnemem Wintu homeland

    The McCloud River arm of this lake is the homeland of the Winnemem Wintu, whose name means middle water people or middle river people. When the lake filled, scores of their village sites, burial grounds and sacred places went underwater. The Winnemem are still here.

    Source: Wikipedia

  9. Exit 698 · on your right going north · History

    The Salt Fire

    On June 30, 2021, a fire started beside I-5 near the Salt Creek exit and shut down the freeway. Forest officials believe it was likely sparked by hot material that fell from a vehicle on the interstate. It destroyed 27 homes and 14 other buildings around Lakehead.

    Source: Wikipedia

  10. Exit 700 · History

    The first federal fish hatchery

    In 1872, the nation's first federal fish hatchery opened on the McCloud River, which now flows into this lake. Livingston Stone built it at a spot where he found Wintu people catching Chinook salmon. Its eggs were shipped across the country and as far as Japan and New Zealand.

    Source: Wikipedia

  11. Exit 702 · on your left going north · Towns

    Lakehead, top of the lake

    Lakehead sits on the northern shore of Shasta Lake. About 470 people lived here in 2020, and in summer more than 1,500 seasonal residents can join them. In 1992, Caltrans finished upgrading a final 13-mile stretch of I-5 north of Lakehead.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 704 to 722Sacramento River canyon

The freeway follows a steep, forested canyon, past old stage stops and Gold Rush camps.

  1. Exit 707 · History

    Delta, end of the line

    When the railroad pushed up this canyon in the 1880s, the town of Delta was the end of the line for about two years. Lots sold for 45 to 185 dollars, and almost overnight there were saloons, hotels, a Wells Fargo office and a turntable for the engines. The 39-mile train ride to Redding took two hours and 20 minutes.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 711 · Land & water

    The upper Sacramento River

    The river beside the freeway is the upper Sacramento. It begins near Mount Shasta, where its North, Middle and South forks meet, passes through Lake Siskiyou, and then runs about 60 miles through this canyon, past Dunsmuir and Castella, to Shasta Lake near Lakehead. Up here it is a blue ribbon trout stream, where wild rainbow trout abound.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 712 · History

    Portuguese Flat

    Near the Pollard Flat exit was Portuguese Flat, a Gold Rush camp of the early 1850s made up largely of Portuguese miners. It had rich diggings and a reputation as one of the roughest camps in northern Shasta County. By 1885 it seems to have been deserted, and its site lies along what is now I-5.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 715 · History

    River, rails and road

    The river, the railroad and the freeway all squeeze through this one canyon. The railroad follows the old Siskiyou Trail. Amtrak's Coast Starlight still runs these tracks every day on its trips between Los Angeles and Seattle.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 718 · History

    Southern's stage station

    The name of this exit goes back to Simeon Southern, nicknamed Sims, who opened a log stage station and hotel here in 1859, where travelers stopped to rest and eat for 50 years. The settlement of Hazel Creek took the name Sims in 1871, since his station was the center of town. In September 1880, President Rutherford B. Hayes stayed here with Generals Sherman and Sheridan.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 720 · History

    The Siskiyou Trail

    Long before this was a freeway, it was a web of Native American footpaths between California and the Northwest. In the 1820s, Hudson's Bay Company fur trappers followed them south from the Columbia River, and the route became known as the Siskiyou Trail. I-5 follows the same path today.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 722 to 735Castle Crags and Dunsmuir

Granite spires rise west of the freeway, and an old railroad town fills the canyon bottom. Dunsmuir gets about 63 inches of rain and snow a year, about three and a half times as much as Yreka or Medford.

  1. Exit 723 · on your left going north · History

    Homeland and the 1855 battle

    The country around Castle Crags is the ancestral home of the Okwanuchu Shasta people. The Crags have long been revered by the Wintu, Achumawi and Modoc peoples too, and the area's streams, river and springs are considered sacred. The Gold Rush brought thousands of miners and terrible violence, and in June 1855 miners fought Modoc people below Battle Rock, now marked at the state park entrance.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 724 · Good stops

    Castle Crags State Park

    In the 1890s, mineral water from a spring here was bottled and shipped all over the world. The bottling company went broke in the 1929 crash, and in 1943 the state bought 925 acres of the old springs property. That was the start of Castle Crags State Park, which has 28 miles of trails and a vista point facing the Crags and Mount Shasta.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 726 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Castle Crags

    These granite spires rise from about 2,000 feet at the river to more than 6,500 feet at the top. The granite formed underground in the Jurassic period, the age of dinosaurs, and Ice Age glaciers later scraped away the softer rock around it. A bellflower that grows here is found nowhere else in the world.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 728 · on your left going north · Good stops

    Sleep in a caboose

    Just off this exit, a lodge founded in 1968 lets guests sleep in old cabooses and eat in a restaurant inside an original train car, with Castle Crags towering above. Once called Railroad Park Resort, it now goes by Jubilee Railroad.

    Source: jubileerailroad.com

  5. Exit 729 · Towns

    Dunsmuir, the railroad town

    Dunsmuir was a railroad town first, nicknamed Pusher, because extra steam engines were added here to push trains up the steep grades north of town. In 1888, Alexander Dunsmuir, son of a British Columbia coal baron, offered the town a fountain if it would take his name. Its slogan calls it the Home of the Best Water on Earth, filtered through Mount Shasta's lava.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 730 · History

    Upper Soda Springs

    Under the freeway in north Dunsmuir lies Upper Soda Springs, now a riverside park. Okwanuchu people camped here during salmon season, at a wide, shallow spot they called the place to cross the river. In the early 1850s it became home to the area's first non-Native settlers, and later to a toll bridge, a stage hotel and a springhouse serving soda water from the mineral springs.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 731 · Oddities

    Babe Ruth in Dunsmuir

    In 1924, New York Yankees stars Babe Ruth and Bob Meusel came to Dunsmuir on a barnstorming tour and played an exhibition game against the locals in the city park. That park is now home to a botanical garden along the river.

    Source: Wikipedia

  8. Exit 732 · Good stops

    Hedge Creek Falls

    A short trail from this exit leads to a waterfall with a small cave behind it, so you can walk behind the falling water. There's also a viewing platform over the river with a view of Mount Shasta. Being so close to I-5, it's a popular stop for passing drivers.

    Source: Wikipedia

  9. Exit 733 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Where Shasta soda began

    Just north of Dunsmuir was Shasta Springs, a summer resort on the railroad main line in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Natural springs here were the original source of the water and drinks that became the Shasta brand of soft drinks.

    Source: Wikipedia

  10. Exit 734 · on your left going north · History

    The Cantara Loop spill

    On the night of July 14, 1991, a train derailed at a horseshoe curve called the Cantara Loop, upstream from Dunsmuir, and a tank car spilled about 19,000 gallons of pesticide into the river. More than a million fish died. It's still the largest hazardous chemical spill in California history, but the river has largely recovered.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 735 to 746Mount Shasta and Black Butte

The canyon opens onto the foot of the volcano, with the city of Mount Shasta, the dark cone of Black Butte and Black Butte Summit.

  1. Exit 736 · on your right going north · Towns

    McCloud, a company town

    Highway 89 heads east to McCloud, a lumber company town on the southern slope of Mount Shasta. For decades the company mill supplied the homes with electricity and even steam heat. The houses passed to private owners in 1965, and the mill closed for good in 2002.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 737 · on your left going north · Orchards & farms

    Mount Shasta trout hatchery

    On Old Stage Road in Mount Shasta, the Mount Shasta Hatchery has raised trout since 1888, and the state calls it the oldest trout hatchery west of the Mississippi. It supplies most of the eggs for the state's trout hatcheries, upwards of 30 million a year. From mid-March through July, visitors can watch the eggs hatch and the baby fish grow.

    Source: wildlife.ca.gov

  3. Exit 738 · on your right going north · Towns

    Mount Shasta City

    This town was called Strawberry Valley, then Berryvale, then Sisson, after Justin Sisson, who gave the land for the railroad station. It officially became the City of Mount Shasta in 1925. It sits at about 3,600 feet, less than 9 miles from the summit, and gets nearly 103 inches of snow in a typical winter.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 739 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Headwaters of the Sacramento

    In Mount Shasta City Park, water pours straight out of a hillside at Big Springs. It started as glacial melt and spent more than 50 years filtering through volcanic rock. About 12 million gallons a day flow out here and help start the Sacramento River.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 740 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Mount Shasta

    Mount Shasta rises to 14,179 feet. By volume, about 85 cubic miles, it is the biggest stratovolcano in the Cascade Range, and seven glaciers ring its slopes. Its last major eruption was about 3,000 years ago, and the U.S. Geological Survey rates it a very high threat volcano.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 741 · Oddities

    The Lemurians legend

    Mount Shasta has a famous legend: a hidden city on the mountain, home to advanced beings from a lost continent called Lemuria. The story grew from a passing mention in the 1880s into a whole book in 1931. No one has ever found the city.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 742 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Black Butte

    The dark cone close beside the freeway is Black Butte, a cluster of lava domes that squeezed out about 9,000 to 10,000 years ago. I-5 crosses a 3,912-foot pass at its western base, called Black Butte Summit.

    Source: Wikipedia

  8. Exit 743 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Shastina and Diller Canyon

    The smaller peak on Mount Shasta's west side is Shastina, about 12,330 feet tall. That's taller than Mount Adams in Washington. The big gash down its side facing Weed is Diller Canyon, cut by hot volcanic flows, and it drops more than 7,000 vertical feet.

    Source: Wikipedia

  9. Exit 744 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Mount Eddy

    The big peak west of the freeway is Mount Eddy, 9,037 feet tall, the highest point in the Trinity Mountains. It's made mostly of a rock called serpentinized peridotite. Mount Eddy is the highest summit west of I-5 anywhere in the United States.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 746 to 756Weed, lumber and wind

Weed grew up around lumber mills. Hot flows from Shastina's eruptions once covered the ground here, and recent wildfires have left their marks.

  1. Exit 747 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Pluto's Cave

    About 12 miles northeast of Weed is Pluto's Cave, a lava tube roughly 190,000 years old. A man named Nelson Cash stumbled onto it in 1863 while looking for stray cattle, and it was named for Pluto, the god of the underworld. Visitors can hike about 1,200 feet inside.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 747 · on your right going north · Towns

    Weed, a windy lumber town

    Weed is named for Abner Weed, who found that the strong winds here were good for drying lumber. In 1897 he bought a mill and 280 acres for 400 dollars. The town motto is Weed like to welcome you, and over the years its road signs have had a habit of disappearing.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 749 · on your right going north · History

    Weed's wildfires

    On September 15, 2014, the wind-driven Boles Fire spread through Weed and destroyed more than 150 buildings, most of them homes, in a single day. On September 2, 2022, the Mill Fire started at the lumber mill north of town and burned the Lincoln Heights neighborhood.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 748 · Oddities

    A two-block highway

    This exit is the end of State Route 265, North Weed Boulevard. It's only two blocks long, which makes it the second-shortest state highway in California.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 751 · Land & water

    Gray wolves return

    Gray wolves have returned to Siskiyou County. In late December 2011, a collared wolf from Oregon known as OR-7 crossed the state line, the first wild wolf confirmed in California since 1924. In 2015 a trail camera in Siskiyou County confirmed the Shasta Pack, two adults and five pups, the state's first resident wolf pack in more than a century.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 755 · History

    Old Highway 99

    A two-lane county road called Old Highway 99 runs between Weed and Yreka, still in the same form it had when it was U.S. 99, the main West Coast highway before I-5. It roughly parallels the freeway, but at one point swings several miles away.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 756 to 770Shasta Valley

A wide, dry valley in the rain shadow of the Klamath Mountains, dotted with hummocks, with hay fields, cattle and juniper.

  1. Exit 759 · on your left going north · Towns

    Gazelle's antelope name

    West of the freeway sits Gazelle, a tiny town of about 95 people. Its post office took the name Gazelle in 1870. A gazelle is a small African antelope, and in the 1850s a steamer called the Gazelle also ran between San Francisco and the upper Sacramento River.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 759 · Land & water

    The hummocks of Shasta Valley

    See the little lumpy hills scattered across the flat valley? Hundreds of thousands of years ago, an older version of Mount Shasta collapsed in one of the largest landslides known on Earth. The debris spread across more than 170 square miles of Shasta Valley, and these hummocks are the pieces.

    Source: usgs.gov

  3. Exit 762 · Orchards & farms

    Hay and cattle country

    Shasta Valley is one of Siskiyou County's four main farming areas. Alfalfa hay is the county's biggest field crop: in 2023, farmers cut about 175,000 tons of it from some 29,000 acres. Ranchers counted around 45,000 cattle and calves at the start of that year.

    Source: siskiyoucounty.gov

  4. Exit 764 · Orchards & farms

    A billion strawberry plants

    Siskiyou County's most valuable farm product isn't hay or cattle. It's strawberry plants. In 2023 the county grew them on more than 2,800 acres, at about 360,000 plants per acre.

    Source: siskiyoucounty.gov

  5. Exit 766 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Shasta Valley Wildlife Area

    About five miles east, near Montague, the state's Shasta Valley Wildlife Area protects some 4,700 acres of juniper woodland, riverside forest, wetlands and fields, with Mount Shasta as its backdrop. Sandhill cranes, waterfowl, birds of prey and shorebirds all use it.

    Source: wildlife.ca.gov

Miles 770 to 785Yreka gold country

Gold Rush country around the county seat, with the Klamath Mountains rising to the west and the Shasta River cutting north toward the Klamath.

  1. Exit 770 · History

    State of Jefferson

    On November 27, 1941, young men waving hunting rifles stopped traffic on U.S. 99 south of Yreka and handed out a proclamation of independence for the State of Jefferson. They promised to secede every Thursday until further notice. The movement's seal is a gold pan with two Xs, called the Double Cross, for feeling abandoned by both state capitals.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 773 · on your left going north · Towns

    Yreka, the gold town

    In March 1851, a mule packer named Abraham Thompson found gold here, and by April, 2,000 miners had arrived at what they called Thompson's Dry Diggings. The name Yreka comes from waik'a, the Shasta people's word for Mount Shasta. Mark Twain told a funnier story: a bakery sign seen backwards, BAKERY without the B, read YREKA.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 776 · History

    Yreka builds its own railroad

    When the Southern Pacific laid its main line through Shasta Valley in the 1880s, it skipped Yreka for a shorter, more level route through Montague. So the people of Yreka built their own railroad. The Yreka Railroad opened in January 1889 and connected the town to the main line at Montague.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 779 · Land & water

    The Shasta River

    The Shasta River starts on Mount Eddy and runs 58 miles through Shasta Valley to the Klamath. North of Yreka, the freeway crosses it, and the river then slips into a canyon. It's a spawning river for Chinook salmon, and estimates say about 80,000 adult fish once returned to it every year.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 781 · History

    The Shasta people

    Shasta Valley and the nearby Klamath River are part of the homeland of the Shasta people, and the people of the Shasta River were known as the Ahotireitsu. Women and children dove in the Klamath for mussels. Today Shasta people live in Yreka and on the Quartz Valley Indian Reservation, and on the Grand Ronde and Siletz reservations in Oregon.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 783 · Land & water

    The Klamath Mountains

    The rugged, forested mountains to the west are the Klamath Mountains. Their rocks began as volcanic islands and pieces of other continents out in the Pacific, some as old as 500 million years, that later crashed into North America. They hold one of the richest mixes of cone-bearing trees on Earth, about 30 kinds.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 785 to 797Klamath River to the Oregon line

Below the old Iron Gate Dam site, the Klamath runs through an arid valley of farms and ranches. To the north rise the Siskiyou Mountains, the divide between the Klamath and the Rogue.

  1. Exit 786 · Good stops

    Randolph Collier Rest Area

    This rest area by the Klamath River honors State Senator Randolph Collier, who lived in Siskiyou County nearly all his life. He co-wrote the 1947 law that laid the groundwork for California's freeway system, so in a way, you're driving on his idea.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 786 · Land & water

    The Klamath River

    The freeway crosses the Klamath River near here, where the river turns sharply south to meet I-5. Upstream, four hydroelectric dams were torn down in 2023 and 2024, the largest dam removal in American history. On October 17, 2024, a fall-run Chinook salmon was spotted in Oregon's Upper Klamath Basin, the first to reach it in more than 100 years.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 787 · History

    Into Karuk country

    Highway 96 follows the Klamath River west into Karuk country. Karuk means upriver, and traditionally the Karuk called themselves the middle of the world people. The Karuk Tribe is one of the largest tribes in California, and the 2010 census counted about 6,100 Karuk people.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 789 · Oddities

    Where Siskiyou got its name

    Nobody is sure where the name Siskiyou comes from. One story says it was a Cree word for a bob-tailed horse. Another says Hudson's Bay Company trappers gave a river ford the French name Six Cailloux, six stones, and that the name was later borrowed for a crossing on the Klamath River near Hornbrook.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 790 · Orchards & farms

    Agricultural inspection at Hornbrook

    Traffic entering California on I-5 stops here, near Hornbrook, at one of 16 agricultural inspection stations that the California Department of Food and Agriculture runs where major highways cross into the state. Inspectors look for pests in plants or cargo that could harm California's farms. Passenger cars with California or neighboring-state plates usually get just a few questions about what they carry.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 796 · on your left going north · Towns

    Hilt, the border town

    Hilt has the northernmost I-5 exit in California. It grew up as a company town, first for the Northern California Lumber Company and then for the Fruit Growers Supply Company, an affiliate of Sunkist. It's named for an early settler, John Hilt.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 797 · History

    The 42nd parallel

    The California and Oregon border follows the 42nd parallel, a line set in an 1819 treaty between the United States and Spain. When California became a state in 1850, it kept that line as its northern border. A survey around 1870 marked it with errors, creating a dispute that continues to this day.

    Source: Wikipedia
