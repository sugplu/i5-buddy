Sacramento to Redding on I-5

From downtown Sacramento (exit 519) to Redding (exit 680) is about 161 miles of I-5 up the Sacramento Valley. Here is what you pass on the way north, in order, with the exit and which side to look.

Driving south instead? Read it from the bottom up; left and right swap.

Miles 519 to 524Downtown Sacramento

I-5 runs between downtown and the river, past Old Sacramento and the Tower Bridge.

  1. Exit 519 · on your left going north · History

    Tower Bridge

    The gold towers just west of the freeway belong to the Tower Bridge, a lift bridge over the Sacramento River dedicated in 1935. Its 160-foot towers hold counterweights as heavy as the 1,150-ton middle span, so just two 100-horsepower electric motors can raise it to let tall boats through.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 519 · Oddities

    The Boat Section

    Through downtown Sacramento, I-5 runs below the water table, close beside the Sacramento River. Caltrans calls this stretch the Boat Section. It was built below street level in the 1960s and 1970s so the freeway would stay out of view of downtown offices and shops, and pumps, drains and a retaining wall keep the river's seepage out.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 519 · Good stops

    California State Railroad Museum

    The California State Railroad Museum, in Old Sacramento, holds 21 restored locomotives and railroad cars, some dating back to 1862. Inside, a large mock-up recreates railroad construction high in the Sierra at Donner Pass around 1867. It features the Central Pacific's first locomotive, the Gov. Stanford, which came to California in pieces by ship, around Cape Horn.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 521 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Where two rivers meet

    The freeway crosses the American River here, close to where it flows into the Sacramento. The park at the meeting of the two rivers, Discovery Park, is designed to flood. In winter high water it can end up underwater, taking pressure off the American River levees.

    Source: regionalparks.saccounty.gov

Miles 524 to 531The airport and the Sacramento River

North of downtown the freeway passes the old ARCO Arena site and the airport and crosses the Sacramento River.

  1. Exit 524 · on your right going north · Oddities

    ARCO Arena, gone but loud

    Just east of the Arena Boulevard exit stood ARCO Arena, home of the Sacramento Kings from 1988 to 2016. Built for just 40 million dollars, it was the cheapest arena in the NBA and the smallest. In 2013 it set a Guinness World Record for the loudest sports roar, at 126 decibels.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 528 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Airport and the big red rabbit

    Sacramento International Airport, just north of the freeway, opened on October 21, 1967, with a single 8,600-foot runway. Inside Terminal B is one of its odder sights: Leap, a 56-foot red rabbit by the artist Lawrence Argent, made to look as if it had jumped into the building from outside. It weighs about 10,000 pounds.

    Source: flysmf.gov

  3. Exit 531 · Land & water

    Crossing the Sacramento River

    The freeway crosses the Sacramento River here, the line between Sacramento and Yolo counties, on the Elkhorn Bridge, built in 1969 and also called the Vietnam Servicemen Memorial Bridge. The Sacramento is the largest river in California. It flows about 400 miles from the Klamath Mountains to the Delta and drains some 26,500 square miles.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 531 to 537Yolo Bypass

Between floods, much of the Yolo Bypass is farmed, mostly in rice.

  1. Exit 531 · Land & water

    Yolo Bypass

    The Elkhorn Causeway here crosses the Yolo Bypass, a floodway 41 miles long and about 3 miles wide. When the Sacramento River runs high, it spills over weirs into the bypass, which can carry about four times the river's own flow and turns into an inland sea. That takes pressure off the levees and lowers the flood risk for Sacramento and Davis.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 536 · on your right going north · Towns

    Knights Landing

    County Road 102 leads north to Knights Landing, on the Sacramento River. Dr. William Knight, a physician from Baltimore, founded it in 1843 on a mound that had long been a meeting place for the Native people living around Cache Creek and the river. It grew into a steamboat landing and a ferry crossing.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 537 to 548Woodland and Cache Creek

The freeway passes Woodland, a farm town with a big tomato cannery, and crosses Cache Creek beside the little town of Yolo.

  1. Exit 537 · on your left going north · Orchards & farms

    Woodland tomato cannery

    The big plant half a mile from the freeway is the Woodland tomato cannery. Tomatoes have been processed and canned here since 1958, under owners including Contadina and Del Monte, and Pacific Coast Producers has run it since 2001. It works in season, generally July through September, and handles about 495,000 tons of tomatoes a year.

    Source: waterboards.ca.gov

  2. Exit 537 · on your left going north · Towns

    Woodland, City of Trees

    Woodland is the county seat of Yolo County and calls itself the City of Trees, for the valley oaks planted around town. In 1853 Henry Wyckoff built a store here called Yolo City. Frank Freeman bought it in 1857, gave land to anyone who would clear it and build a home, and in 1859 suggested the name Woodland.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 541 · on your left going north · History

    Capay Valley, Yocha Dehe homeland

    Highway 16 heads west past Esparto into the Capay Valley, which Cache Creek flows through. The valley is the traditional home of Patwin, or southern Wintun, people, and today it is home to the Yocha Dehe Wintun Nation, a federally recognized Patwin tribe. The tribe is headquartered in Brooks, in the valley, where it runs the Cache Creek Casino Resort.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 541 · Land & water

    Cache Creek, the trappers' creek

    The freeway crosses Cache Creek here. Its name comes from Hudson's Bay Company fur trappers, who cached, or hid, their furs along the Sacramento River and its smaller streams. They called this one Riviere la Cache.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 542 · on your right going north · Towns

    Yolo, place of rushes

    The little town of Yolo shares its name with the county, and the name comes from the Patwin language. Nobody is quite sure of its source. It may come from Yo-loy, said to mean a place abounding in rushes, from a village called Yodoi near Knights Landing, or from that village's chief, Yodo.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 548 to 560Zamora and the Dunnigan Hills

Farm country continues past Zamora and Dunnigan, near the Dunnigan Hills, and on clear days the Sutter Buttes rise from the valley floor to the north.

  1. Exit 548 · Orchards & farms

    Sunflower seed fields

    Some fields in this part of the valley grow sunflowers for planting seed, not for snacks. California has historically produced about 95 percent of the hybrid sunflower seed planted in the United States, and a quarter of the world's supply, with seed fields in Yolo County and elsewhere in the Sacramento Valley. Bees carry pollen between interplanted rows of male and female plants to make the hybrid seed.

    Source: agalert.com

  2. Exit 548 · Towns

    Zamora, cut by the highways

    The farm community of Zamora was once called Black's, after J. J. Black, who settled here in 1865. When the Northern Railway built its line north from Woodland in the 1870s, Black donated a strip of land for a depot, and a little town grew up around the station, with a hotel, stores, a jail, blacksmith shops and six warehouses.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 553 · History

    I-505, once I-5W

    Interstate 505 meets I-5 here and runs south to I-80 at Vacaville. It was first planned as part of I-5W, a western loop of Interstate 5 through the Bay Area, while the route through Sacramento was to be I-5E. The split numbering was dropped, and the western loop became parts of I-580, I-80 and I-505.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 556 · on your right going north · Towns

    Dunnigan, once Antelope

    Dunnigan began as Antelope. In the winter of 1853, a flood washed two settlers, Copp and Wilson, off their claims near the Sacramento River, and they moved up to higher ground here. The following year a Yolo County postal worker named A. W. Dunnigan arrived and went into the hotel business with Henry Yarick.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 559 · Land & water

    Yellow-billed magpie

    Watch the fields and tall trees along here for the yellow-billed magpie, a black-and-white bird with a bright yellow bill and a small patch of yellow skin around each eye. It lives only in California, in the Central Valley and the foothills and mountains beside it. In good light its black feathers shine green, blue or purple.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 560 to 578Almonds, rice and the Sutter Buttes

Almond orchards ring Arbuckle and rice fields spread across the flat valley floor, while the Sutter Buttes rise from the plain to the east.

  1. Exit 566 · on your right going north · Oddities

    College City, no college

    About two miles east is College City, home to about 290 people and no college at all. The town began with a school, Pierce Christian College, paid for with money from the estate of Andrew Pierce. The college opened in 1875 and left town in 1896.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 566 · Towns

    Tacitus Arbuckle's town

    Arbuckle was laid out in 1875 and named for Tacitus Arbuckle, on whose ranch it grew. To get a town going, he gave lots to anyone who would build on them, and he became its first postmaster. The first train pulled in on July 4, 1876, just two months after the depot was finished.

    Source: countyofcolusaca.gov

  3. Exit 567 · Orchards & farms

    Almond orchards

    The long, straight rows of trees around here are mostly almond orchards. In 2024 almonds were Colusa County's most valuable crop, worth about 279 million dollars, from roughly 63,000 acres. Almond pollination season usually runs from about mid-February to mid-March, when the trees bloom with fragrant white to pale pink flowers before their leaves come out.

    Source: countyofcolusaca.gov

  4. Exit 572 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Sutter Buttes, closest view

    On clear days, the cluster of peaks rising from the valley to the east is the Sutter Buttes, centered about 17 miles away. They are the worn remains of a volcano that was active roughly a million and a half years ago, and they are often called the world's smallest mountain range. The Maidu, Nisenan and Wintun names for them all roughly mean the Middle Mountains.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 575 · History

    Patwin homeland

    The western Sacramento Valley is the homeland of the Patwin, the southern branch of the Wintun peoples. Their language has two main dialects: River Patwin, spoken along the Sacramento River, and Hill Patwin, in the hills and plains to the west. Today Patwin people are citizens of three federally recognized tribes, including the Kletsel Dehe Wintun Nation, headquartered in Williams.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 577 · on your left going north · Good stops

    Granzella's in Williams

    In Williams, Granzella's has served the public since 1976. The family business includes a restaurant, deli, bakery and sports lounge, and it sells hand-packed jars of olives. Guy Fieri featured it on the Food Network, including its muffuletta sandwich.

    Source: granzellas.com

Miles 578 to 597Rice fields and wildlife refuges

Rice fields spread across the valley here, and the marshes of the Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge sit just east of the freeway.

  1. Exit 578 · on your right going north · Towns

    Colusa and Korusi

    Highway 20 leads about 8 miles east to Colusa, the county seat, on the Sacramento River. Its name comes from Korusi, a Patwin village that stood on the west bank of the river, where the city is today. In the 1850 law that created the county, the name was spelled Colusi, and newspapers often wrote Coluse.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 582 · Orchards & farms

    Rice country

    Many of the flat fields around here are rice paddies. In 2024, Colusa County harvested rice from about 118,000 acres, roughly 185 square miles, for a crop of more than half a million tons. Rice was the county's most valuable crop in 2023, and second only to almonds in 2024.

    Source: countyofcolusaca.gov

  3. Exit 586 · on your left going north · Land & water

    Sites Reservoir, a lake to come

    In a valley in the hills west of Maxwell, a new lake is planned: Sites Reservoir. Instead of damming a river, it would be filled with water taken from the Sacramento River during high flows, carried by canals and pumped up into the valley. Full, it would hold up to 1.5 million acre-feet, enough to serve 7.5 million people for a year.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 588 · History

    Swift's Stone Corral

    About 6 miles west of Maxwell stands Swift's Stone Corral, a state historical landmark since 1936. In 1850, rancher Granville Swift and his partner Frank Sears needed a corral, but there was no timber anywhere around, so they built one out of the flat stones scattered over the land.

    Source: ohp.parks.ca.gov

  5. Exit 588 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Delevan refuge

    About 5 miles east of the freeway lies Delevan National Wildlife Refuge, nearly 5,800 acres of managed marsh and upland. Each winter more than 200,000 ducks and 100,000 geese come to the refuge. Visitors can watch wildlife from a simple roadside overlook on the Maxwell-Colusa Highway.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 595 · on your right going north · History

    Rancho Larkin's Children

    East of here, along the river around Princeton, lay a land grant made out to children. Thomas Larkin, the United States consul in Monterey, would not become a Mexican citizen, so he could not get land himself. In 1844 he had three of his children naturalized, and two days later the children received a grant of about 44,000 acres.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 595 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge

    The marshes just east of the freeway are the Sacramento National Wildlife Refuge, established in 1937. Fall is the big show. On the refuge's own calendar, waterfowl counts average about 1.5 million in October and 1.8 million in November, when geese arrive by the thousands.

    Source: fws.gov

Miles 597 to 619Willows and Glenn County

North of Willows, almond orchards grow around Artois.

  1. Exit 601 · Land & water

    Mendocino National Forest

    The mountains on the western skyline are the Coast Range, home of the Mendocino National Forest, which has its headquarters in Willows. The forest covers about 913,000 acres in six counties, and it is the only national forest in California without a major paved road entering it.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 603 · on your right going north · Towns

    Willows, the cattle stop

    Willows takes its name from a shady watering place ringed by willow trees, where cattle drivers traveling through the valley stopped to rest. The post office opened in 1862 as Willow, singular, and did not add the s until 1916. The town has been the seat of Glenn County since the county was created in 1891.

    Source: cityofwillows.org

  3. Exit 607 · History

    Hugh Glenn, the Wheat King

    Glenn County is named for Hugh Glenn, a doctor who came west in the Gold Rush and became California's Wheat King. His land along the Sacramento River grew to 55,000 acres. At harvest he hired 150 men, and on August 8, 1879, his crews brought in 5,779 bushels of wheat in a single day.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 610 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Artois, once Germantown

    Artois was called Germantown until 1918. Local belief holds that during World War One, a troop train stopped here for water and the soldiers took offense at the German name. Petitions to change the post office name succeeded, and on May 21, 1918, Germantown became Artois, after the battles fought in that region of France.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 614 · Orchards & farms

    Prune orchards

    Some of the orchards in the northern Sacramento Valley are prune plums, grown to be dried. Tehama and Glenn are among California's leading prune counties, and California grows more than 90 percent of the nation's prunes and about 40 percent of the world's. Harvest runs from mid-August into early September and usually takes about 30 days.

    Source: cdn.agclassroom.org

  6. Exit 619 · on your right going north · Orchards & farms

    Orland, Queen Bee Capital

    Orland calls itself the Queen Bee Capital of North America. In 2017 the city council adopted the title because of how many queen bees the town's locally owned commercial apiaries produce. Orland even has a museum about bees, the Honeybee Discovery Center, and it holds a Queen Bee Festival every June.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 619 to 636Orland and Corning orchards

Orchards of almonds, walnuts, prunes and olives grow around Orland and Corning, and the freeway crosses Stony Creek and Thomes Creek.

  1. Exit 619 · History

    Will Green's water notice

    East of Orland, on the Sacramento River, lies Hamilton City. Nearly 4 miles north of town, on December 18, 1883, Will S. Green posted a notice on an oak tree on the river's west bank, claiming 500,000 miner's inches of river water to irrigate the west side of the valley. The spot is now a state historical landmark.

    Source: ohp.parks.ca.gov

  2. Exit 621 · Land & water

    Stony Creek

    The freeway crosses Stony Creek just north of Orland. The creek was named for the loads of rock and sediment it once washed down from the mountains in floods, and it was once called the Capay River. Today Black Butte Dam, built in 1963 in the foothills to the west, traps most of that sediment.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 628 · on your left going north · History

    Paskenta Band of Nomlaki

    Rolling Hills Casino, beside the freeway, belongs to the Paskenta Band of Nomlaki Indians, the Hill Nomlaki people of this part of the valley. In 1959 the federal government terminated the tribe's rancheria and its land was sold. The Paskenta Band kept its identity and culture and won back full tribal status in 1994.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 628 · Orchards & farms

    Walnut orchards

    Many of the orchards in Tehama County are English walnuts. In 2024 the county had about 31,000 acres of bearing walnut trees, worth about 95 million dollars, its second most valuable crop after almonds. California grows 99 percent of the commercial walnuts in the United States.

    Source: tehama.gov

  5. Exit 630 · on your right going north · History

    Lassen's rancho and Stanford's vines

    About 8 miles east, near Vina, lay Rancho Bosquejo, the land grant of Peter Lassen, the Danish blacksmith whose name is on Lassen Peak. In 1848 he led emigrants to his ranch over a new route, the Lassen Cutoff. Signs his agents later put up promised Gold Rush travelers that the diggings were only 110 miles away, but the cutoff proved about 200 miles longer than the main trails.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 631 · on your right going north · Towns

    Corning, olive town

    Corning began in 1882, when the railroad was extended to this point, and it was named for John Corning, a railroad official. Its biggest local industry is growing and preparing table olives. Corning is home to Bell-Carter Foods, maker of Lindsay Olives and the largest table olive processor in the United States.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 633 · Good stops

    Helmick rest area

    These rest areas are named for Lieutenant John C. Helmick, the California Highway Patrol's Red Bluff Area commander. On February 27, 1989, while driving to speak at a Rotary Club luncheon in Oroville, he was killed when his car crashed into a Caltrans truck parked on the shoulder of Highway 99. He was 43.

    Source: chp.ca.gov

  8. Exit 633 · Land & water

    Thomes Creek

    The freeway crosses Thomes Creek here, named for Robert Thomes, a pioneer who arrived in the 1840s. His name has been misspelled so often that official records list the creek as Thomas Creek, Thoms Creek and Toms Creek. The creek runs about 62 miles from the Coast Range to the Sacramento River, with no permanent dams.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 636 to 649Tehama County to Red Bluff

This is the northern end of a band of farmland that runs almost unbroken to Bakersfield, and on clear days Lassen Peak stands on the eastern skyline.

  1. Exit 636 · on your right going north · Towns

    Tehama, the first county seat

    About 5 miles east, on the Sacramento River, is the little city of Tehama, home to 435 people in 2020. When Tehama County was formed in 1856, Tehama was its county seat, but a county-wide election moved the seat to Red Bluff the very next year. Because the town floods easily, its houses must sit above flood level, so most have tall stairways to the front door.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 642 · on your right going north · Towns

    Gerber, the railroad stop

    East of the freeway, Gerber was platted in 1910 and named for the Gerber family, who owned the town site. From 1916 to 1965 it was a division point on the Southern Pacific Railroad, where steam locomotives took on fuel, got repairs and changed crews. The town also had livestock yards and an icing plant for refrigerator cars.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 642 · History

    Ishi and the Yahi

    The foothill canyons east of here, along Mill and Deer creeks, are the ancestral homeland of the Yahi, the southernmost group of the Yana people. After the Gold Rush, settlers killed nearly all of the Yahi; in 1865, about 40 were killed in the Three Knolls Massacre on Mill Creek. A few survivors hid in the canyons for decades.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 647 · on your right going north · History

    Red Bluff Diversion Dam

    East of the freeway, the Red Bluff Diversion Dam, a concrete structure nearly 6,000 feet long, crosses the Sacramento River. Finished in 1964, it raised the river to feed the Tehama-Colusa and Corning canals, which water about 150,000 acres of farms. But it also blocked salmon, so since 2013 its gates have been locked open to let the river run free.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 649 · on your left going north · History

    John Brown's widow

    In the fall of 1864, Mary Brown, widow of the abolitionist John Brown, arrived in Red Bluff with her children, poor and homeless. The town helped her, and a fund headed by Governor Frederick Low raised 450 dollars for a lot and a small house on Main Street. That house is now a California Historical Landmark.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 649 to 662Red Bluff rangeland

Between Red Bluff and Cottonwood, mildly rugged rangeland separates the valley's crop areas.

  1. Exit 649 · on your left going north · Towns

    Red Bluff, head of navigation

    Red Bluff is named for red bluffs that once stood out along the banks of the Sacramento River, which the freeway crosses twice in town, less than two miles apart. The town grew up at the head of navigation, the farthest point riverboats could reach, and miners landed here bound for the gold fields of Trinity County. It was first called Leodocia, then Covertsburg.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 650 · on your right going north · History

    William B. Ide Adobe

    A mile north of Red Bluff, on the west bank of the Sacramento River, a state historic park honors William Ide, the only president of the California Republic. That republic, born in the Bear Flag Revolt, lasted from June 14 to July 9, 1846, less than a month. Here is the twist: research later showed that Ide never owned or lived in the adobe that bears his name.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 651 · History

    Alvin Coffey's freedom

    Red Bluff was home to Alvin Coffey, born enslaved in Kentucky in 1822. In 1849 his enslaver forced him to California to mine gold, then took him back east and sold him. Coffey returned to the gold fields, mined enough to buy freedom for himself and his family, and in 1857 the family set out for California and settled in Red Bluff.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 653 · on your right going north · Oddities

    Bend, once Horsethief Bend

    East of the freeway lies the little river community of Bend. It was first called Horsethief Bend, then Sander's Bend, and finally just Bend, after a loop in the Sacramento River nearby. About 600 people live there today.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 653 · Good stops

    Herbert S. Miles rest area

    These rest areas are named for Herbert S. Miles, a highway engineer who spent 42 years working on California's state highways. As a district engineer, he helped lead the creation of the state's Safety Roadside Rest program. A plaque honoring him stands at the rest area on the east side of the freeway.

    Source: waymarking.com

  6. Exit 657 · Land & water

    Mount Shasta from afar

    On clear days, the lone peak far off on the northern skyline is Mount Shasta, about 80 miles away. It rises abruptly, towering nearly 9,800 feet above the land around it. On a clear winter day, it can be seen from the floor of the Central Valley as far as 140 miles to the south.

    Source: Wikipedia

  7. Exit 662 · Oddities

    Cottonwood truck scales

    The truck scales here are the Cottonwood Commercial Vehicle Enforcement Facility, run by the California Highway Patrol. Officers and inspectors check trucks and their drivers against North American safety standards and state law. The first station, on one side of the freeway, opened in 2000, and a second, for trucks going the other way, opened in 2025.

    Source: chp.ca.gov

Miles 662 to 675Cottonwood and Anderson

Near the northern tip of the Central Valley, the freeway passes Cottonwood and Anderson, with ranch land along Cottonwood Creek.

  1. Exit 662 · Land & water

    Cottonwood Creek

    The freeway crosses Cottonwood Creek here, which forms the line between Tehama and Shasta counties along its whole length. It is the largest undammed tributary of the Sacramento River, which is why it still has runs of Chinook salmon and steelhead. John C. Frémont named it in 1846, describing cottonwoods along its sandy, gravelly bed.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 664 · on your right going north · Towns

    Cottonwood, the halfway town

    Cottonwood was a stagecoach town, settled in 1849, and it got its first post office in 1852. It sits halfway between Redding and Red Bluff, 15 miles from each, on both banks of Cottonwood Creek, which gave it its name. About 6,300 people live here now, nearly twice as many as in 2010.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 665 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Coleman fish hatchery

    About 7 miles east, on Battle Creek, is Coleman National Fish Hatchery, the largest salmon hatchery in the continental United States. It was built in 1942 to make up for spawning grounds lost when Shasta Dam blocked the river. Its goal is to raise about 12 million fall-run Chinook salmon each year.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 668 · on your right going north · Land & water

    Lassen Peak

    On clear days, the big mountain on the eastern skyline is Lassen Peak, about 40 miles away, the southernmost active volcano in the Cascade Range. On May 22, 1915, it blasted a column of ash more than 30,000 feet into the sky. A photograph of that eruption was taken from Red Bluff, about 45 miles from the peak.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 668 · on your left going north · Towns

    Anderson, a railroad town

    Anderson is a railroad town. In 1872, a rancher and hotel keeper named Elias Anderson deeded the Central Pacific Railroad a right of way through his land, and in return the railroad named its depot after him. Today Anderson is a city of about 11,000, and it is the headquarters of Sierra Pacific Industries.

    Source: Wikipedia

  6. Exit 673 · on your left going north · History

    Reading's gold on Clear Creek

    Clear Creek joins the Sacramento River just west of the freeway, south of Redding. Five miles up the creek, at a spot called Reading's Bar, Pierson B. Reading and his Native workers found gold in 1848. Both the creek and Reading's Bar are now state historical landmarks.

    Source: ohp.parks.ca.gov

  7. Exit 673 · on your left going north · History

    Redding Rancheria

    West of the freeway, south of Redding, is the Redding Rancheria, a federally recognized tribe of Wintu, Pit River and Yana people. Its 31-acre site was bought by the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 1922, as a place to camp and live for Native people who had been made landless by settlers.

    Source: Wikipedia

Miles 675 to 680Redding

Redding sits at the northwestern end of the Central Valley, with mountains on the north, east and west and farmland to the south.

  1. Exit 675 · History

    The 1846 river massacre

    On April 5, 1846, an armed expedition led by Captain John C. Frémont attacked a large Wintu camp on the banks of the Sacramento River near what is now Redding. The Wintu, most of them women and children, were trapped against the river. Estimates of the dead range from 125 to 900, and no one was ever punished.

    Source: Wikipedia

  2. Exit 677 · History

    The Carr Fire

    On July 26, 2018, the Carr Fire jumped the Sacramento River into the west side of Redding, and 38,000 people had to evacuate. That evening a fire whirl formed, with winds estimated above 143 miles an hour, as strong as an EF3 tornado, and it rose an estimated 18,000 feet into the sky.

    Source: Wikipedia

  3. Exit 678 · on your left going north · Towns

    Redding, once Poverty Flats

    During the Gold Rush, miners found little gold in this ground, and the spot was called Poverty Flats. In 1868 the Central Pacific Railroad's land agent, Benjamin Redding, bought land here for a northern terminus, and the railroad built a town and named it for him. The tracks arrived in 1872.

    Source: Wikipedia

  4. Exit 678 · on your left going north · Oddities

    Sundial Bridge

    West of the freeway, the Sundial Bridge, a 700-foot footbridge designed by Santiago Calatrava, crosses the Sacramento River. Its 217-foot mast points due north at a 42-degree angle and casts its shadow on a giant dial. The tip of that shadow moves about a foot a minute, so you can watch the Earth turn.

    Source: Wikipedia

  5. Exit 680 · on your left going north · History

    Old Shasta

    Highway 299 leads to Shasta, about six miles west of Redding. In the 1850s it was the largest town in the area and the county seat, home to some 3,500 people. Then in 1873 the railroad bypassed it for Redding, and Shasta faded into a near ghost town.

    Source: Wikipedia
